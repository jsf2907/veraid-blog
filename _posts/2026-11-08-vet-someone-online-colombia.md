Knowing how to vet someone online means confirming their identity and checking that their story holds up, through reverse image searches, video calls, verified profiles, and payment trail checks, before you meet in person. This guide gives you separate workflows for dating matches and for drivers, tour guides, and other bookings, each with its own red flags and escalation steps.

## What does it actually mean to vet someone online?

Vetting someone online is the process of confirming that the person you're about to meet or hire is who they claim to be, using evidence you gather before you're standing in front of them. In practice it means cross-checking their name, photo, and details across independent sources rather than taking one profile at face value.

### Verification vs. trust: a match is not a verification

Trust is a feeling; verification is evidence. A match on a dating app, a five-star listing, or a friendly WhatsApp voice note tells you nothing about identity. A verified identity comes from checking details the person cannot easily fake, like a government-issued ID checked by a verification service, or a live video call where they match their photos.

### What vetting can and cannot tell you

Vetting can confirm identity and consistency. It cannot tell you a person's intentions, and scammers exploit exactly that gap: the [FTC's guidance on romance scams](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/what-know-about-romance-scams) describes how imposters build rapport over weeks before asking for money. Treat vetting as a filter, not a guarantee.

## How do you properly vet someone before meeting or hiring them?

Vetting properly means running a person through five layers, in order, before you commit to a meeting or a payment. Skipping layers or doing them out of order is where most travelers get caught.

### The five-layer vetting process

1. **Identity:** confirm the person is who they claim to be. A photo is not a verified identity; ask for a video call, and for a guide, driver or host, look up their TRU ID before you meet.
2. **Consistency:** compare what they say across channels. A driver who quotes one price on WhatsApp and another in person is telling you something.
3. **Footprint:** search their name, phone number, and business name. Real tour guides and operators leave traces; fresh accounts with no history do not.
4. **Transaction safety:** never pay the full amount upfront to a stranger. Deposits to a traceable method beat wire transfers to a personal account.
5. **Meeting design:** choose a public, populated location for a first meeting and tell someone where you are going.

### Why the order of steps matters

Each layer filters out a different type of problem, and later layers assume earlier ones passed. Verifying payment details for someone whose identity you never confirmed just documents a scam. A friendly video call does not replace a footprint check. Work the layers in sequence and stop at the first one that fails, because a person who fails layer one has no business reaching layer five.

## How do you vet someone you met on a dating app?

### Dating vetting workflow: profile to first meetup

Start with the profile: reverse-image search their photos through a tool like [TinEye](https://tineye.com/), then video-call before agreeing to meet. Treat every claim (job, hotel, neighborhood) as unconfirmed until you see it live on camera.

For the first meetup, pick a public place you chose, tell a friend where you are, and share a check-in time with them. Save Colombia's emergency number, 123, in your phone before you go.

### Red flags specific to dating apps in a travel context

Watch for anyone who pushes to move off the app immediately, refuses a video call, claims to be nearby but never is, or steers the first meeting to a private apartment rather than a public spot. A profile with few photos, no linked social presence, and a story that changes between chats is a pattern problem, not a personality quirk. In-app verification badges help, but self-reported claims prove nothing.

### Safe escalation steps if something feels off

Unmatch and block first; you owe no explanation. Then report the profile through the app so the account gets reviewed. If you already met and something felt wrong, tell your hotel or a friend, change your plans for the rest of the day, and keep the chat screenshots in case you need them later. If you are in danger, call 123.

## How do you vet a driver, tour guide, or service provider before paying?

### Professional vetting workflow: booking to arrival

Start before money moves. Ask for the provider's full name, the company or platform they work under, and a booking confirmation in writing. Cross-check the name against the platform profile, ask for their TRU ID if they have one, and confirm the price, route, and duration in the same message thread.

Book through a platform rather than a direct transfer when you can, so there is a record and a refund path. Pay a deposit, not the full amount, and keep payment inside the platform if it offers that. Before arrival, agree on a check-in: a photo of the vehicle or meeting point sent in advance, so you can confirm the person who shows up matches the person you vetted.

### Red flags specific to hiring and bookings

Watch for pressure to move payment off-platform, a price that changes after you agree, refusal to send a photo of the vehicle or credentials, and a profile created recently with no reviews. A friendly chat proves nothing about who holds the licence.

### Escalation steps for transactional disputes

If the provider no-shows or changes terms, stop payment, document the thread with screenshots, and file a dispute through the platform first. If you paid by card, you can ask your bank about a card dispute, but there are deadlines and eligibility depends on your bank. Report the profile so the next traveler sees it.

## Which online vetting red flags should make you stop immediately?

Some signals mean pause and dig deeper. Others mean close the app and walk away. The difference is whether the person is hiding something inconvenient or actively steering you toward a bad outcome.

### Hard stops vs. soft warnings

### Printable red-flag checklist

- No video call accepted across two attempts.
- Profile photos reverse-search to someone else.
- Any request for money, gift cards, or crypto.
- Excuses change each time you ask the same question.
- Pressure to meet quickly, alone, at night, or off-platform.

Screenshot everything before you unmatch. If two or more hard stops appear, the vetting is done: the answer is no.

## What free tools and searches can you use to vet someone online?

### Reverse image and name searches

Run their profile photos through Google Images or TinEye before you meet. Scammers reuse stolen pictures, and a match whose face appears on three unrelated profiles is telling you something. Search their first name plus the city, and their app handle, to see whether the same identity shows up consistently across platforms.

### Cross-referencing profiles and reviews

For drivers and tour guides, look for the same name and phone number on multiple channels: a booking platform profile, a WhatsApp Business listing, recent guest reviews. A profile on one platform means little; consistency across several is the free version of verification. For dates, check that their social accounts are old enough to have history, not created last month with forty followers.

### Limits of free vetting

Free searches confirm consistency, not truth. A polished scam can pass every reverse image check, and no search tells you whether the person standing at the meeting point is the person you messaged. That gap is where an ID-verified lookup helps.

## When should you escalate from DIY vetting to a verification service?

### Signs DIY vetting has hit its ceiling

Reverse image searches and social cross-checks stop working when a profile is thin, newly created, or built on photos that simply don't appear anywhere. If someone refuses a video call, changes their story about where they live or work, or your driver or guide has no traceable business presence, you've exhausted what free searches can tell you. Before you travel, check the [US State Department's Colombia travel advisory](https://travel.state.gov/en/international-travel/travel-advisories/colombia.html) for the current level and the regions it flags; it is a reasonable nudge to add a stronger check before meeting anyone in person.

## How do you vet someone online without crossing legal or privacy lines?

### What you can and cannot do with someone's data

You can search publicly available information: social profiles, business registrations, platform reviews, and anything the person has posted publicly. You cannot impersonate them, hack accounts, pay for leaked data, or use someone else's identity documents. Recording a video call they agreed to is different from covertly recording one. If a vetting step requires breaking a platform's terms or a country's data rules, skip it.

The line is simple: use what someone chose to make public, and never what they expected to keep private. When in doubt, ask the person directly rather than digging through channels they did not consent to.

### Respecting the other person's privacy too

Vetting cuts both ways. Keep what you find to yourself, and don't share screenshots of someone's profile beyond what you need to report a problem. A TRU ID lookup shows only a photo, first name, and verification status, not a dossier. If a guide or driver can't show any verification and won't do a video call, that is information, not an accusation.

## FAQ

### Can you vet someone online without them knowing?

Yes, for the passive parts: reverse image searches, reading reviews, and checking business registrations all happen without the other person's involvement. The active parts, like video calls, require their cooperation. A complete vet needs both, since searches confirm consistency while a live call confirms the person matches their photos.

### Is it safe to send a photo of my passport to get verified on an app?

Treat any document upload as a trust decision about the app, not just the match. Verify the app is a real, established company before submitting anything, and never send identity documents directly to a stranger over WhatsApp. Legitimate verification happens inside a platform, not through a personal message thread.

### How can I verify a tour guide or driver in Colombia is legitimate?

Ask for their full name and company, request booking confirmation in writing, and cross-check both against their platform profile and recent reviews. If they have a TRU ID, look it up in VeraID to see their photo, first name, and verification status. Pay a deposit through the platform rather than the full amount by transfer, and ask for a photo of the vehicle or meeting point in advance.

### What should I do if someone refuses to verify their identity before we meet?

Treat refusal as information, not an accusation, but hold your position: no verification, no meeting. Offer an alternative like a live video call where they match their photos. If their excuses change each time you ask, stop the conversation, block the account, and report it through the app.

### Do dating apps actually verify profiles, or is it just a badge?

Both exist, and they are not equivalent. Some apps run photo verification where you submit a selfie or short video matched to your photos, which is a real check at signup. A badge still only proves who joined, not who shows up, and self-reported claims prove nothing. Treat any badge as one layer, never the whole vet.
