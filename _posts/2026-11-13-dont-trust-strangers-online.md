You can decide whether an online stranger is worth trusting by checking four signals: identity verification, consistency over time, incentive disclosure, and third-party corroboration. This article maps those signals to the situations you'll actually face in Colombia, from dating app matches to tour guides to "friendly locals" in your DMs, with per-scenario checklists and named failure modes.

## Why 'Don't Trust Strangers Online' Is Bad Advice Without a Method

Blanket distrust doesn't work. You'll still book the tour, still meet the match, still reply to the friendly DM. The US State Department has an active [travel advisory for Colombia](https://travel.state.gov/en/international-travel/travel-advisories/colombia.html) (check the current level before you go), but the real problem is that "be careful" gives you no way to distinguish one stranger from another.

What works instead is a method: check four signals before you commit. Is the person's identity verified? Have they been consistent over time? Do they disclose what they gain from you? Can a third party corroborate their story? Charm is not evidence. The next section turns those four signals into a repeatable framework.

## The Four Signals: A Framework for Judging a Stranger Online

You cannot read intentions, but you can evaluate four things before you ever agree to meet: whether their identity is verified, whether their story holds steady over time, whether they've disclosed what they gain from you, and whether anyone else can confirm their claims.

### Signal 1: Verified identity

A profile photo and a first name prove nothing on their own. Verified identity means the person has passed a check that ties them to a real government document, ideally one they chose to complete rather than one forced on them at signup.

### Signal 2: Consistency over time

Compare what they told you last week with what they say today. Do their job, neighborhood, and plans stay the same, or do details drift? Scammers and catfishers improvise; consistent people don't have to.

### Signal 3: Incentive disclosure

Everyone you meet while traveling has a stake: commissions, tips, romance, or just company. Trustworthy people say so upfront. Vagueness about who pays them, or a "friend's" tour that suddenly costs money, is a signal in itself.

### Signal 4: Third-party corroboration

Can a second source confirm the first? A tour guide whose company has a listed address, a driver whose plate matches the app, a match whose name and face appear on another account with history.

<div class="vb-table" markdown="1">

| Signal | What it confirms | What it can't confirm |
|---|---|---|
| Verified identity | They are who they claim | Their intentions |
| Consistency | Their story holds together | That the story is true |
| Incentive disclosure | Why they want your time | That they'll honor it |
| Corroboration | Others vouch for claims | That vouching is honest |

</div>

No single signal is decisive. All four together give you something close to a real answer.

## How Do I Vet a Dating App Match in Colombia?

Before meeting, run four checks: does their identity hold up (a video call where they match their photos, or a verified profile), are they consistent over time (same job, same story, same details a week apart), do they disclose their incentive (why they're actually talking to you), and can a third party corroborate them (the same name and face on another account with history). If two of the four fail, postpone the meetup.

### Failure mode: the rushed meetup

Urgency is the oldest lever in imposter scams. The [FTC's guidance on romance scams](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/what-know-about-romance-scams) describes scammers who move the relationship forward fast and always have a reason they can't meet or video chat. A match who can only meet tonight, or who steers you somewhere private immediately, is asking you to skip the consistency and corroboration signals entirely. Asking for a video call first is a normal request; treat a refusal as information.

### Failure mode: the off-platform pivot

The move to WhatsApp or Telegram within the first few messages isn't always sinister, but it removes the one place where reporting is easy and history is visible. Stay on the app until you've met, keep the chat as your record, and tell one person where you're going and with whom. A message to that friend afterward costs you nothing and closes the loop.

## How Do I Know a Tour Guide or Driver Is Real?

Run a guide or driver through the same four signals before you pay a deposit: verified identity, consistency over time, incentive disclosure, and third-party corroboration. A real operator passes all four. A fake passes one, maybe two.

### Failure mode: the too-good price

A price far below every comparable tour is the incentive disclosure working in reverse. The operator's incentive is your deposit, not the trip. If a full-day tour costs half of what every listing and hostel board quotes, ask yourself what is being subsidized. Usually it is your urgency.

### Failure mode: the borrowed reviews

Reviews on a guide's own WhatsApp status or Instagram highlights are easy to fabricate. Third-party corroboration means reviews on a platform you can cross-check, posted by accounts with history, over months rather than one enthusiastic week. Match the name in the reviews to the name on the booking.

Ask a guide or driver for their TRU ID before sending money, then look it up yourself in [VeraID](https://veraid.org/). A friendly voice note is not verification. If someone resists every check, that resistance is your answer.

## What About DMs, Marketplace Sellers, and 'Friendly Locals'?

The same four signals apply, but casual contact strips away the paperwork that dating apps and booking platforms provide. A DM from someone claiming to be a local has no verified profile, no platform holding identity documents, and no review history. That means you lean harder on consistency over time and incentive disclosure: does their story stay the same across days, and are they clear about what they want from you?

Marketplace sellers sit in between. You usually have a transaction history and reviews, which is third-party corroboration, but identity verification is often optional. Ask for some form of identity check before paying a deposit, and treat a refusal as a signal, not an insult.

### Failure mode: the urgency play

The pattern to end immediately is manufactured urgency: a limited-time price, a driver "already on the way," a seller who must close tonight. Urgency exists to stop you from running the four signals. And do not confuse having a location-sharing or SOS app on your phone with having verified who you are meeting: those are different jobs.

## Red Flags That Should End the Conversation

Some behaviors are disqualifying no matter how many other signals line up. If someone refuses any identity check but demands your passport photo, hotel name, or a deposit through a payment app, the asymmetry is the answer. If their story changes between platforms (different name, employer, or city than what a reverse image search or their profile shows), that inconsistency is not a detail to overlook. Watch for urgency paired with isolation: a driver who insists on a route change, a match who pushes to move somewhere private early, a "friendly local" who discourages you from bringing anyone else. Pressure to skip a check you would normally expect is itself the signal.

## Your Pre-Meetup Checklist

Run the four signals in order before you leave your accommodation:

1. **Identity verification.** Has the person completed any form of verified identity, such as a TRU ID you can look up, or are you relying on a photo and a first name?
2. **Consistency over time.** Do their details hold steady across days of chat, or do names, jobs, and plans shift?
3. **Incentive disclosure.** Are they honest about what they gain, a commission, a tour booking, a referral, or do they dodge the question?
4. **Third-party corroboration.** Can a booking platform, reviews with history, or a second account confirm who they are?

If any signal fails, reschedule over video or meet in a public spot during daylight. If two fail, end the conversation.

## FAQ

### Is verification actually worth asking for, or is it just about collecting IDs?

It is worth asking for because a person who has chosen to verify is giving you a signal. With VeraID, the guide, driver, host or professional submits their ID to VeraID, not to you, and you only see their photo, first name and verification status. You learn two things at once: whether they are who they say, and whether they are willing to be checked.

### Can someone fake a verified profile?

Faking one is much harder than faking a photo or a first name, because verification ties a person to a real government document and a live biometric check. That does not make it impossible, which is why verification is only signal one of four. Treat a verified identity as confirming identity, then still check consistency, incentives, and corroboration before you commit.

### How do I verify someone without asking for their passport?

You do not need their passport. If they have a TRU ID, look it up in VeraID and compare the photo and first name with the person you are talking to. Beyond that, use a video call where they match their photos, and look for the same name and face on a booking platform or a second account with history.

### What should I do if a guide or match refuses any check?

Treat the refusal as information, not an insult. Someone who won't do a quick video call or share a way to confirm who they are, before asking for your time or a deposit, is telling you something. If they also dodge questions about who pays them, end the conversation.

### Does verification mean the person is safe to meet?

No. Verification answers one question only: is this person who they claim to be? It tells you nothing about intent, history, or behavior after the check. A verification is not a character reference. Run all four signals, meet in public, and tell someone where you are going.
