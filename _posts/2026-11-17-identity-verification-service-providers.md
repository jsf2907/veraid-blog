Identity verification matters for service providers because it answers the one question every traveler asks before handing over money or getting in a car: is this person actually who they claim to be? For guides, drivers, and hosts, a verified identity turns a stranger into a known contact, protects payouts, and earns repeat bookings. For travelers, it closes the gap between booking a name on an app and meeting a real person.

## Why does identity verification matter for service providers?

Verification protects both sides of a service transaction. The traveler booking a tour or driver replaces a claimed identity with a checked one, and the guide or driver being booked can prove they are who their profile says they are. A match on an app is not a verification.

The principle is established in regulated industries: banks and other financial institutions must identify and verify their customers as part of customer due diligence, and the FATF has published guidance on how digital identity can support that ([FATF Guidance on Digital Identity](https://www.fatf-gafi.org/media/fatf/documents/recommendations/pdfs/Guidance-on-Digital-Identity-report.pdf)). Service providers meeting strangers face a similar problem, just without a bank's controls.

At [VeraID](https://veraid.org/), the person being met (a guide, driver, host or professional) verifies their government ID with a biometric check plus a human review and gets a permanent TRU ID. The traveler looks up that TRU ID before the meeting and sees the provider's photo, first name and verification status. Visitors never verify their own ID.

## What counts as identity verification for a service provider?

Verification is a tiered thing, and most confusion comes from treating the bottom tier as the top one.

### What a profile photo does and does not prove

A profile photo proves nothing on its own. Anyone can upload a picture of a smiling guide pulled from a search result, and the traveler booking the tour has no way to tell. A photo is a claim. Verification is evidence behind the claim. Matching the person who shows up to the person in the photos still tells you only that they look like their pictures, not that their name, license, or business is real.

### Document check plus liveness: the minimum bar

The minimum bar is a government-issued document check plus a live selfie match, done once, voluntarily, with the result available to the people who need to check it. That combination shows that one real person holds one real identity, which is what a traveler needs to know about a driver or guide before a first meeting.

## What happens when a tour guide or driver skips verification?

### The booking that looked legitimate

A traveler finds a guide on a Facebook group or WhatsApp referral, pays a deposit, and shows up at the meeting point. The profile photo was borrowed, the reviews were from friends, and the name on the bank transfer belongs to someone else. Because the guide's identity was never checked, there is no verified record to compare against and no trail showing who agreed to what. This is a common pattern: the US FTC says imposter scams have been the most frequently reported fraud category since 2020, with more than 1 million reports and over $3.5 billion in reported losses in 2025 ([FTC, March 2026](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2026/03/ftc-testifies-joint-economic-committee-agencys-efforts-combat-fraud)).

### What recourse exists when identity was never checked

Very little. Without a verified identity, a traveler has little to put in a police report, a payment platform cannot confirm who received the funds, and the report may name a person who does not exist. The failure hurts honest providers too: legitimate guides lose bookings because travelers have no simple way to check a tour guide's identity. Verification fixes this before the meeting, not after.

## How does identity verification reduce fraud in service businesses?

Fraud in service businesses clusters around money changing hands with an unverified counterparty. A traveler pays a deposit, a platform pays out a driver, and nobody can prove who the person on the other end is.

### Fake operator accounts and impersonation

A scammer creates a listing that copies a real tour operator's photos and name, collects deposits, and disappears. The traveler loses money; the real operator absorbs the angry reviews for a business they never ran. A verified identity tied to one ID number gives the real operator something a copy cannot fake: a traveler can look up the TRU ID and see whether the photo and name match the person they are talking to.

### Payouts to the wrong person

When a platform or operator pays out without a verified identity, a changed bank detail or a hijacked account redirects the money. Disputes then land on a counterparty who cannot be located. Verifying the provider's identity before the first payout means everyone knows the person receiving the money is the person the record says.

## Which regulations require service providers to verify identity?

Most identity verification obligations sit in regulated categories such as financial services and money transfer, where KYC-style rules require businesses to confirm who a customer is before transacting. Independent tour guides and drivers usually fall outside those rules, which is exactly why voluntary verification fills the gap. A traveler booking a tour has no rule that guarantees the guide is who they claim to be. Rules vary by country; this is general information, not legal advice.

Voluntary verification turns that compliance logic into a trust signal: a verified identity tells the traveler that the guide, driver or host checked out before the booking. For travelers, that means knowing who they are meeting before they go. For providers, it means repeat bookings from clients who trust what they see.

## How do service providers protect themselves when meeting clients?

A driver picking up a stranger from a night flight, a guide meeting a solo traveler at a trailhead, a host handing over keys: each faces an unknown-person problem too. VeraID does not verify visitors, so providers need their own habits.

Confirm the booking in writing with the client's full name and contact number, take deposits through a payment method that leaves a record, and meet first in a public, busy place. Share the meeting point, time and client details with someone you trust, and agree on what happens if either party is late. ## How do you verify before you meet in Colombia?

The workflow is the same whether you're booking a tour, hiring a driver, or staying with a host: verify before you meet.

First, ask for the TRU ID. Ask the guide, driver or host for their TRU ID before you pay a deposit. A friendly chat is no proof at all.

Third, tell someone where you are going, who you are meeting, and when you expect to be done.

## FAQ

### Is identity verification the same as a background check?

No. A background check looks up records tied to a name, such as legal history, and requires access to official databases. Identity verification shows that one real person holds one real identity through a document check and a live selfie match. Verification answers "who is this person?"; record searches answer "what has this person done?". Travelers meeting guides and drivers usually need the first.

### Can I use a dating app match's profile as proof of identity?

No. A profile photo, a first name, and a chat history are claims, not evidence. Anyone can build an account with borrowed pictures and a plausible story. For a dating match, a live video call before meeting is the simplest check. For a guide, driver or host, ask for their TRU ID and look it up before paying a deposit or meeting.

### Do I have to verify my identity to book tours in Colombia?

Usually not, and with VeraID you never verify your own ID: you only look up the provider's TRU ID. Independent guides and drivers generally sit outside KYC-style rules, so it is up to you to ask for proof of who they are before you pay. Rules vary; this is general information, not legal advice.
