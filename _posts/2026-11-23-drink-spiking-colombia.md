Drink spiking in Colombia is a real risk that the UK, Canadian and U.S. governments all warn travelers about, mostly linked to robbery. You can lower your risk a lot with a few habits: keep your drink with you, don't accept food, drinks or cigarettes from people you just met, meet dates in busy public places, and always tell someone where you are. Most cases in official warnings start with a stranger or a new match the traveler had no way to check.

## How common is drink spiking in Colombia?

There is no reliable public count of spiking cases, because many people never report them and some don't remember what happened. What we do have are clear, repeated warnings from governments that track problems their citizens run into.

The UK Foreign Office says the British Embassy "has received reports of criminals in Colombia using drugs to subdue their victims", including scopolamine, which "temporarily incapacitates victims" ([UK FCDO, Colombia safety and security](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/colombia/safety-and-security)). Canada's travel advice says scopolamine can leave victims "disoriented and unable to make clear decisions for 24 hours or more" ([Government of Canada, Colombia travel advice](https://travel.gc.ca/destinations/colombia)). The U.S. State Department's country page also warns about scopolamine in food and drinks ([U.S. State Department, Colombia country information](https://travel.state.gov/content/passports/en/country/colombia.html)).

So the honest answer is: it isn't something that happens on every night out, but it happens often enough that three governments describe it in detail. That's reason enough to build a few habits.

## How does drugging usually happen?

The official warnings describe a common pattern: criminals often work in teams, target people who are alone, and use the drug to make the victim easy to rob.

According to the UK Foreign Office, drugs can be given "through food, drinks, cigarettes, aerosols and even paper flyers", and incidents have been reported "in nightclubs, bars, restaurants, public transportation, including taxis, and on city streets." Canada adds that drugs can be put into food and drinks and other everyday items, and that criminals "often target those travelling alone."

The common thread is trust. Something is offered by a person you just met, or your drink is left where someone else can reach it. You don't need to memorize every method. You need a few rules that cover all of them.

## Are dating apps part of the problem?

Yes, according to official advice. The UK Foreign Office says "criminals use dating applications and websites to identify and lure foreign nationals who are travelling alone and looking to meet local people." Canada reports "an increase in deaths related to online dating applications used by criminals to identify and lure foreigners", naming Bogotá, Cartagena and Medellín, and says victims were drugged with scopolamine or another incapacitating drug.

A U.S. Embassy security alert reported eight suspicious deaths of U.S. citizens in Medellín over two months and warned about the risks of meeting people through dating apps ([Travel Weekly](https://www.travelweekly.com/South-America-Travel/US-warns-Americans-dating-app-danger-Colombia)).

This doesn't mean you can't date while traveling. Most matches are who they say they are. It means a match is a starting point, not proof, and the first meeting should be set up so that a bad actor gets nothing from it.

## How do I keep my drink safe at a bar or club?

These habits come straight from official advice, plus common sense:

- **Never leave your drink unattended.** If you go to the bathroom or the dance floor, finish it first or leave it and order a new one.
- **Watch it being made.** Order at the bar and take it from the bartender yourself.
- **Don't accept drinks, snacks, gum or cigarettes from new acquaintances.** Canada's advice specifically says to be wary of "snacks, beverages, gum, cigarettes or anything else from new acquaintances or someone in the street." A polite "no thanks, I'm good" is enough.
- **Pick drinks you can control.** A bottle you opened yourself or a glass you hold is easier to keep an eye on than a shared pitcher.
- **Pace yourself.** It's harder to notice something is off when you've had a lot to drink. The UK also notes it's illegal to drink alcohol in public spaces in Colombia.
- **Stick with your group.** Agree before you go out that nobody leaves alone or with someone they just met.

## How do I plan a safe first date in Colombia?

Most of the risk can be removed before you even meet.

**Video call first.** A short live video call shows you the person matches their photos and is willing to be seen. Someone who always has a reason not to video call is giving you useful information.

**Choose the place yourself.** Pick a busy café, restaurant or bar you know, in an area you're comfortable with. Canada advises avoiding meetings in isolated locations.

**Get there and back on your own.** Use your own ride from a source you trust, not a car your date sends or a taxi hailed on the street. The UK advises using pre-booked or app-based taxis, or booking through your hotel.

**Tell a friend.** Canada advises telling a family member or friend your plans, "including details of with whom and where your date is." Share the match's profile, the venue and the time, and your live location.

**Keep it in public.** Both the UK and Canada advise not bringing new acquaintances back to your accommodation, and the UK adds not to accept invitations to theirs.

**Bring only what you need.** Leave your passport, spare cards and expensive watch at your accommodation. Carry one card, some cash and your phone.

## What are the warning signs that something is off?

Some warning signs are about the person, and some are about how you feel.

**Signs about the situation:**

- Your match pushes to move the date to a private place, a party "with friends" or their apartment.
- They avoid a video call but are keen to meet fast.
- A new friend keeps buying rounds or insists you try something.
- People you just met pay unusual attention to your phone, cards or where you're staying.

**Signs about how you feel:** feeling far more drunk than you should for what you've had, sudden confusion, dizziness or drowsiness are all reasons to stop and get help. The UK warns these drugs can cause loss of consciousness and memory loss and can be dangerous in large amounts. Don't try to push through it, and don't leave with the people you're with.

## What should I do if I think I've been drugged?

This is general information, not medical or legal advice. If you think you or a friend may have been drugged:

1. **Get to safety.** Tell bar staff or a trusted friend right away and stay in a public place.
2. **Call 123.** It's Colombia's emergency number for police and ambulance.
3. **Get medical help.** The UK Foreign Office advises getting local medical attention as soon as possible.
4. **Don't go anywhere with the people you just met**, even if they offer to "take you home."
5. **If you're robbed, don't resist.** Canada advises handing over cash, devices and valuables without resistance, because criminals may use weapons.
6. **Report it.** You can report crimes to the Colombian police online through ADenunciar ([Policía Nacional](https://www.policia.gov.co/noticia/una-herramienta-al-servicio-ciudadania-adenunciar)) or in person, and contact your embassy. U.S. citizens can find embassy contacts on the [State Department's Colombia page](https://travel.state.gov/content/passports/en/country/colombia.html).
7. **Lock your accounts.** Call your bank to block cards and change passwords if your phone was taken.

Before you travel, check the current U.S. travel advisory level for Colombia on the [State Department advisory page](https://travel.state.gov/en/international-travel/travel-advisories/colombia.html), since levels change.

## FAQ

### Is drink spiking common in Colombia?

There is no reliable count, but the UK, Canadian and U.S. governments all warn that criminals in Colombia use drugs such as scopolamine in food and drinks to rob people, especially travelers who are alone or meeting someone new.

### What is scopolamine?

It's a drug that official travel advice says criminals use to incapacitate victims. Canada says it can leave people disoriented and unable to make clear decisions for 24 hours or more.

### Is it safe to use dating apps in Colombia?

Many people do, but official advice says criminals use dating apps to target foreigners. Video call first, meet in a busy public place, arrange your own ride, tell a friend, and don't bring new acquaintances back to your accommodation.

### What number do I call in an emergency in Colombia?

Call 123 for police and ambulance. Then get medical help and contact your embassy if you're a foreign visitor.
