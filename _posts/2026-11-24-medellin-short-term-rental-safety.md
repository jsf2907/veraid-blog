## Key takeaways

- Most rental scams start with a copied or fake listing and a request to pay outside the booking platform. Keep messages and payment on the platform.
- A price far below similar places, pressure to decide fast, or a host who "can't meet" are warning signs. So is a request for wire, gift card or crypto payment.
- In Colombia, short-term rental hosts (vivienda turística) need a Registro Nacional de Turismo (RNT). It's fair to ask your host for theirs.
- Plan the key handoff before you land: who meets you, where, and at what time. Don't hand the door over to someone you can't identify.
- If your host has a VeraID TRU ID, look it up before arrival to see their photo, first name and verification status.

To book a short-term rental in Medellín safely, book through a platform that holds your payment, never pay or move the chat outside it before booking, and check that the listing and host look consistent. Before you arrive, confirm in writing who will hand you the keys and where. Most problems start when a traveler sends money to, or opens the door for, someone whose identity was never confirmed.

## Why check your host before arriving in Medellín?

Short-term apartments are a popular way to stay in Medellín. That demand attracts honest hosts and, sometimes, people who copy their listings.

The U.S. Federal Trade Commission explains how this works: scammers "copy the pictures, description, or virtual tour from a real online rental listing," or post ads for places that aren't for rent or don't exist ([FTC, rental listing scams](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/rental-listing-scams)). In a city you don't know yet, a copied listing can look completely real.

There is also a personal safety side. A host or "building manager" will know when you arrive, where you sleep and that you're new in town. Canada's travel advice for Colombia tells visitors not to bring new acquaintances back to their accommodation and, when meeting someone from a dating app, to tell a friend who they're meeting and where ([Government of Canada, Colombia travel advice](https://travel.gc.ca/destinations/colombia)). Knowing who holds the key to your door is part of the same habit.

## What are the signs of a fake rental listing?

The FTC's rental and vacation rental pages list the same warning signs again and again ([FTC, rental listing scams](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/rental-listing-scams); [FTC, vacation rental scams](https://consumer.ftc.gov/consumer-alerts/2018/07/getting-vacation-rental-watch-out-scams)):

- **The price is too good.** A premium apartment at a price far below similar places nearby.
- **Pressure.** The "owner" says other people want it and you must pay today. The FTC's advice: "If anyone pressures you to make a decision quickly, walk away."
- **The owner is away.** The fake owner usually claims to be out of the country and can't show the place.
- **The same address, a different owner.** If other ads show the same apartment under a different name or company, the FTC calls that a sign of a scam.
- **Unusual payment.** Wire transfer, gift cards or cryptocurrency only. The FTC says plainly that if someone insists on these, "it's a scam."

A quick check helps: search the listing's photos and the host's name online and see whether the same apartment appears elsewhere under another name.

## Should I ever pay a Medellín host outside the booking platform?

No, not before you're there and not to secure the booking. Booking platforms build their protections around payment on the platform. For example, Airbnb's help center says that paying outside it means you lose its AirCover protection and that a request to pay by wire or bank transfer may be a fraudulent reservation ([Airbnb, paying and communicating through Airbnb](https://www.airbnb.com/help/article/209/paying-and-communicating-through-airbnb)). Its policy also says hosts and guests may not ask each other to pay outside the platform and lists requests to pay by wire or bank transfer as signs of fraud ([Airbnb, pricing and payment transparency](https://www.airbnb.com/help/article/199/paying-outside-airbnb)).

Watch for the classic move: a host who says, "Message me on WhatsApp and I'll give you a better price by bank transfer." That takes you out of the system that could refund you.

The FTC adds two points that apply to any rental: get a copy of the contract before you send any deposit, and remember that "Wiring money, sending gift cards, or paying by cryptocurrency is the same as sending cash" ([FTC, rental listing scams](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/rental-listing-scams)). Once that money is gone, it is very hard to get back.

## What is the RNT, and should my host have one?

In Colombia, tourism service providers register in the Registro Nacional de Turismo (RNT). The City of Medellín's guide for short-term rentals in residential buildings says hosts need an RNT specific to vivienda turística, renewed every year between January and March. It also lists other duties, such as the building's rules expressly allowing tourist rentals, registering every guest on the national accommodation registration card (TRA), and carrying liability insurance that covers guests ([Alcaldía de Medellín, viviendas turísticas infographic](https://www.medellin.gov.co/es/wp-content/uploads/2026/11/infografico-viviendas-turisticas.pdf)).

For a traveler, this gives you a simple, polite question: "What is your RNT number?" A host running a real, registered business should have one. The Medellín guide names the Cámara de Comercio de Medellín para Antioquia and the Ministry of Commerce, Industry and Tourism (MinCIT) as the places for RNT verification.

The rules for tourist rentals are also being reviewed. As of July 2026, MinCIT had reopened a draft decree that keeps the RNT mandatory and adds stronger controls for platforms ([El Colombiano, July 20, 2026](https://www.elcolombiano.com/negocios/decreto-registro-nacional-turismo-airbnb-hoteles-vivienda-turistica-FK39000647)). This is general information, not legal advice; the details can change.

## What should I ask my host before I book?

Good hosts are happy to answer clear questions. Ask through the platform's messages, so there is a record:

- The exact address, or at least the building and neighborhood, so you can check it exists on a map.
- The host's full name and, if different, the name of the person who will meet you.
- Their RNT number.
- How check-in works: in person, a building reception, or a lockbox.
- House rules, including the building's rules for guests and visitors.
- The cancellation policy and what happens if the place doesn't match the listing.

Vague, changing answers, or a push to "just send the deposit and I'll explain later," are reasons to keep looking.

## How do I plan a safe key handoff in Medellín?

The handoff is the moment the listing becomes a real person at a real door. Plan it before you fly:

1. **Confirm who will meet you.** Ask for their name and a photo through the platform. If a cleaner or manager will hand over the keys, ask for their name too.
2. **Agree on the time and place.** Most buildings have a doorman or reception. Meeting in the lobby is easier than on a dark street.
3. **Arrange your ride from the airport.** Official advice for Colombia is not to hail taxis on the street and to use a dispatch service, an app or a pickup arranged in advance ([U.S. State Department, Colombia country information](https://travel.state.gov/content/passports/en/country/colombia.html)).
4. **Share the details.** Send a friend the address, the host's name and your arrival time.
5. **Check before you go up.** If someone you weren't told about says they're "the manager" and asks for cash or your passport details, pause and message the host on the platform first.

## What if something feels wrong when I arrive?

Trust that feeling. If the apartment is not the one in the photos, the person at the door is not who you were told, or someone asks for extra cash on the spot, don't go in. Step back to a public place, contact the platform's support, and book somewhere else if needed. Report fake listings to the website where you found them.

If you lost money, the FTC suggests reporting to the website, the police and [ReportFraud.ftc.gov](https://reportfraud.ftc.gov/) for U.S. travelers ([FTC, rental listing scams](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/rental-listing-scams)). In Colombia, you can file some police reports online through ADenunciar ([Policía Nacional, ADenunciar](https://www.policia.gov.co/noticia/una-herramienta-al-servicio-ciudadania-adenunciar)). If you feel unsafe at any time, call 123, Colombia's emergency number.

## FAQ

### Are short-term rentals in Medellín safe?

Many travelers stay in them without problems. The main risks are fake or copied listings, payment outside the platform and not knowing who will meet you. Booking through a platform, checking the host and planning the key handoff lowers those risks.

### Is it a red flag if a host asks me to pay by bank transfer?

Yes, before booking it is. Booking platforms tell guests to pay only through the platform and treat requests for wire or bank transfers as possible fraud. The FTC says wire, gift card and crypto payments are like sending cash.

### Do short-term rental hosts in Colombia need to be registered?

According to the City of Medellín's guide, hosts offering vivienda turística need an RNT for that activity and must renew it every year between January and March. Asking for the RNT number is a reasonable check. This is general information, not legal advice.

### What should I do if the apartment doesn't match the listing?

Don't move in. Take photos, contact the platform's support right away, and find another place to stay if needed. Report the listing to the platform.

### Can VeraID check a host who doesn't use the app?

No. VeraID only works when the host has verified and has a TRU ID. For hosts without one, keep the booking and payment on the platform, ask for their RNT number and confirm who will meet you in writing.
