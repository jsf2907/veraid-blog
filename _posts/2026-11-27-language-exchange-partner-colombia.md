The safest way to meet a language exchange partner in Colombia is to start at a group event in a public venue, then move to one-to-one practice only after a short video call. For the first meeting alone, pick a busy café in daylight, set a time limit, keep alcohol out of it and tell a friend where you'll be. Most people you meet this way just want to practice English and make a friend, and these habits keep it that way.

## Why are language exchanges so popular in Colombia?

Visitors, remote workers and students in Colombia often want to learn Spanish, and many Colombians want to practice English. Language exchanges put the two together: you speak Spanish for part of the time and English for the rest.

Some events are big. One weekly Spanish and English exchange group in Medellín lists more than 5,800 members on Meetup and meets every Tuesday afternoon, followed by a party later in the evening ([Meetup, Medellín Spanish and English Language Exchange](https://www.meetup.com/medellin-spanish-and-english-language-exchange-meetup)). Smaller café meetups, university clubs and one-to-one partners from language apps are also common in Bogotá, Cartagena and other cities.

That mix is what makes exchanges fun. It's also why a little planning helps: you're meeting strangers, often in the evening, sometimes in places where drinks are flowing.

## Is a group event or a one-to-one partner safer?

For your first few exchanges, a group event is the easier choice. You're in a public venue, there are organizers and dozens of other people around, and you can move tables or leave at any time without explaining yourself.

A one-to-one partner can be great for real progress, but it needs more care. You're meeting one person you know only from chat. Treat that first meeting the way you would any meeting with someone from the internet, and use the steps below.

A good path looks like this:

1. **Start at a group event** and get a feel for the scene.
2. **Swap contacts only with people you clicked with**, and only what you're comfortable sharing.
3. **Video call before meeting alone**, especially if the partner came from an app rather than an event.
4. **Make the first one-to-one meeting short and public.**

## How do I check a partner before meeting one-to-one?

One large language exchange app puts it simply in its own tips: always video chat beforehand, ideally a few times, before meeting a partner in person ([Tandem, tips for face-to-face tandems](https://tandem.net/blog/tips-tandem-partner-irl)). It also suggests watching for inconsistencies in the person's story and being careful if they seem interested in something other than learning languages.

A short call tells you a lot:

- **Do they match their photos?** Same face, similar age, same general look.
- **Can they actually speak the language they offered?** A partner who claims to be a fluent English speaker and can't hold a basic conversation is worth questioning.
- **Do the details hold?** Their city, studies or job should match what they said in chat.
- **How do they react to a normal plan?** A genuine partner will be happy with a café in the afternoon.

It's also worth doing a reverse image search of their profile photo. The U.S. Federal Trade Commission recommends this as a way to spot photos taken from someone else ([FTC, what to know about romance scams](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/what-know-about-romance-scams)).

## Where should a first meeting with a language partner happen?

Choose a neutral, busy, well-lit place such as a café, and meet in the daytime. The same language app recommends avoiding alcohol at the first meeting and agreeing on a time limit in advance, around an hour ([Tandem](https://tandem.net/blog/tips-tandem-partner-irl)). An hour is plenty for practice, and it gives both of you an easy end point.

A few more habits that make a big difference:

- **Get there and back on your own.** Use a ride from an app or a dispatch service rather than hailing a taxi on the street ([U.S. State Department, Colombia country information](https://travel.state.gov/content/passports/en/country/colombia.html)).
- **Tell a friend** who you're meeting, where and when, and agree on a time to check in. Lonely Planet's advice for meeting fellow travelers says the same, and suggests having an easy exit excuse ready ([Lonely Planet](https://www.lonelyplanet.com/articles/meeting-up-with-other-travellers-safety-tips)).
- **Watch your drink and your food.** Don't leave them unattended, and don't accept anything opened by someone you just met.
- **Keep your accommodation private** for now. Don't share your exact address or room number, and don't invite a new partner back, or go to theirs, on a first meeting.

Australian police give similar advice to anyone meeting someone from online: pick a place with lots of people around, meet in daylight and share your location with friends ([Victoria Police](https://www.police.vic.gov.au/safety-tips-when-meeting-someone-from-online)).

## What if a language exchange starts to feel like a date?

It happens, and it isn't automatically a problem. Two adults can meet to practice Spanish and find they like each other. What matters is that you're both honest about it and that you stay in control of the pace.

It helps to be clear early. A simple "I'm here to practice Spanish, and I'd love to keep doing that" sets expectations without being rude. If the other person keeps pushing toward a date when you've said you just want practice, that's a reason to step back.

If you do want to see where things go, treat the next meeting like any first date. Official travel advice for Colombia warns that criminals use dating apps and websites to target foreign visitors traveling alone, and advises not to accept invitations to a stranger's accommodation or invite them to yours ([UK government, Colombia safety and security](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/colombia/safety-and-security)). Canada's advice also tells travelers to avoid isolated meetings and to tell a friend who they're meeting and where ([Government of Canada, Colombia travel advice](https://travel.gc.ca/destinations/colombia)). Keep it public, keep your own ride and keep a friend in the loop.

## What red flags should I watch for?

Most language partners are exactly who they say they are. These patterns, though, are worth noticing, especially when several show up together:

- **They avoid video calls** or always have a reason not to show their face.
- **They push for a private place** for the first meeting, or ask which hotel or apartment you're in.
- **The story keeps changing**: their job, city or reason for learning English shifts from chat to chat.
- **They ask for money** for any reason: a class fee before you've met, a bill, a ticket, an "investment". The FTC's advice is clear: never send money or gifts to someone you haven't met in person ([FTC](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/what-know-about-romance-scams)).
- **They rush things**, with strong feelings in a few days or pressure to keep talking off the app.
- **They want you to drink a lot**, or to move from the café to a second, quieter place.

You can always end a chat or leave a meeting. A polite "I need to head off, thanks for the practice" is enough.

## What should I do if something goes wrong?

If a partner asks for money, threatens you or makes you feel unsafe, stop replying and don't send anything. Then:

- **Report the profile** on the app or group where you met them, and tell the event organizers if you met at an event.
- **In an emergency in Colombia, call 123.**
- **File a police report online** through the Policía Nacional's ADenunciar service ([Policía Nacional](https://www.policia.gov.co/noticia/una-herramienta-al-servicio-ciudadania-adenunciar)).
- **Report scams** to the FTC at [ReportFraud.ftc.gov](https://reportfraud.ftc.gov/) if you're in the U.S., or to the fraud authority in your home country.
- **Tell someone you trust.** It's easier to think clearly when you talk it through.

This is general information, not legal advice.

## FAQ

### Are language exchange events in Colombia safe?

Group events in busy, public venues are a low-key way to meet people, and most attendees are there to practice and socialize. Use the same care as any night out: watch your drink, keep your own ride home and leave when you want to.

### Should I video call a language partner before meeting?

Yes, if you're meeting one-to-one for the first time. A short call shows that the person matches their photos and can talk naturally, and it filters out most fake profiles. A good call is a good sign, not a guarantee, so still meet in public.

### How long should a first language exchange meeting be?

About an hour is a good length. It's enough time for real practice, and a set end time makes it easy for either of you to leave.

### What if my language partner asks me out?

That's your choice. If you're not interested, say so kindly and clearly. If you are, plan it like any first date: a public place, your own transport, a friend who knows where you are, and no visits to each other's accommodation at first.
